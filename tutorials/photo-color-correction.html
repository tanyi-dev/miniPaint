Photo Color Correction Basics: Rescue Dull, Flat Photos

By Web PSEditor • Difficulty: Beginner • 7 min read • Updated: 13 Nov 2026

Phone photos come out flat for a reason. The camera exposes for the highlights so nothing burns out, then applies a conservative contrast curve that leaves room for later edits. The result protects detail and looks grey. A quick pass of brightness, contrast and saturation fixes most of it — but move the sliders in the wrong order and you spend the time making it worse.

The Result: Before vs After

What Auto Adjust Actually Does

It is not a smart filter. The tool multiplies every channel until about 1% of the pixels sit at or above near-white, then does the same at the dark end aiming for roughly 0.5% near-black — repeating up to ten times until that quota is met. In plain terms, it stretches the tonal range so the image uses the full 0–255 span instead of a narrow band in the middle.

That is exactly right for a mid-tone photo shot in even light, and wrong for two common cases. A scene that already contains white paper, snow, or a bright sky has its 1% of near-white pixels before the tool starts, so the stretch lands on the mid-tones and pushes them up: the sky clips and the colours go pastel. A night shot has almost no near-white pixels, so the tool keeps multiplying and turns black into grey noise.

Because it multiplies rather than offsets, it also raises saturation slightly as a side effect. On a portrait that is already warm, that is the difference between natural skin and an orange cast.

Check the Histogram First

Open Image → Histogram before touching anything. The graph tells you which problem you actually have:

  • Everything bunched in the middle, both ends empty — genuinely flat. Auto Adjust is appropriate.
  • Left edge piled up against zero — crushed shadows, detail already gone. Lifting brightness will only reveal noise, not detail.
  • Right edge clipped flat — blown highlights. No slider recovers them; the only fix is to expose darker next time.
  • Spike in one colour channel — that channel is clipping before the others, which is almost always a colour cast rather than an exposure problem.

The Sliders, In the Right Order

Image → Color Corrections opens one dialog with six groups. The order below matters more than the values, because each step changes what the next one is looking at:

1. Fix the colour cast first (Red / Green / Blue)

Each channel slider runs −255 to +255. Find something in the frame that should be neutral — white paper, a grey wall, a white shirt — and adjust the channel that is too strong downward. A photo shot under tungsten light usually has too much red and not enough blue; nudge Red down and Blue up in small steps and watch the neutral area lose its tint.

2. Brightness, then contrast

Brightness runs −100 to +100, contrast the same. Lift brightness until the mid-tones look right, then add contrast until the image stops looking hazy. Doing it in the other order means the contrast stretch amplifies the lift and clips the highlights. On the sample, +8 brightness and +15 contrast is enough.

3. Saturation last, and cautiously

Saturation also runs −100 to +100. Stop at the point where the colour looks right on the most saturated object in the frame, not the average — a red mug reaches its limit well before a beige wall does. Faces turn orange first, so check skin at 100% zoom before confirming.

4. Luminance and Hue, for specific problems

Luminance moves brightness without touching colour saturation, which is useful on a photo where lifting brightness washed out the colour. Hue rotates every colour at once (−180 to 180 degrees) — a blunt tool, but the fastest way to bring a strongly green underwater or fluorescent-light shot back toward neutral before fine-tuning the channels.

When a Boost Is Not Enough

If the flatness comes from haze rather than exposure, no slider fixes it cleanly — lifting contrast adds mud before it adds depth. Two options that work better on this kind of image: Effects → Vibrance, which lifts muted colours less aggressively than the saturation slider, and a light Effects → Sharpen pass after resizing, which restores the local contrast that haze removed. Both are one-shot operations, so duplicate the layer if you want to compare.

What this editor does not have is worth knowing up front: there are no curve controls, no separate temperature and tint sliders, and no local adjustments by selection — every correction applies to the whole layer. Severe casts are therefore a channel-balancing job, and selective edits mean duplicating the layer and erasing back. That limitation shapes the workflow more than any slider value.

Export and Keep the Original

Finish with File → Export as JPG at quality 85 or higher. Keep the untouched original file: corrections are baked into the pixels when you confirm, so re-editing means starting from the original rather than from the exported JPEG. If the photo will be printed or edited again later, export PNG instead — every JPEG save throws away a little more of the gradient detail you just recovered.

Quick Answers

Auto Adjust made the photo worse. Why?

It pushes the image until roughly one percent of pixels sit near white and half a percent near black. That works on a mid-tone photo and fails on a scene that already contains bright sky or white paper, because the tool has nowhere to push and clips the highlights instead. Check the Histogram first: if either end is already touching the edge, skip Auto Adjust and work the sliders by hand.

Brightness first, or contrast?

Brightness first, then contrast, then saturation, and colour casts before any of that. Raising contrast first stretches the tonal range, so a later brightness lift pushes the highlights straight into clipping. And saturation magnifies whatever cast is already there, which is why a colour cast needs the RGB channels before you touch saturation at all.

The same file looks different on my phone. Which screen is right?

Neither is authoritative. Phone screens ship with different gamma curves, wider or narrower colour gamuts, and an auto-brightness setting that changes with the room. A file that looks correct on a calibrated monitor can look washed out on a phone at 30% brightness. Judge the image against a neutral grey area on the same screen rather than chasing how it looks on one specific device.

💡 Order of operations: cast → brightness → contrast → saturation. Auto Adjust only when the histogram has empty room at both ends.
